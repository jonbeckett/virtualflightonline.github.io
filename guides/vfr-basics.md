# VFR basics

VFR stands for **Visual Flight Rules** — the set of regulations that govern flying when visibility is sufficient to navigate by sight. Most VFO group flights for GA aircraft and beginners are conducted under VFR.

> All content on this page is for entertainment purposes in flight simulation only and is not intended for real-world use.

## The fundamental principle

Under VFR, you are responsible for **see and avoid** — meaning you maintain separation from other aircraft, terrain, and obstacles by looking out the window. There are no radar controllers directing your every move. You navigate by reference to the ground and sky.

## Visibility and cloud clearance

VFR flight requires:

- Sufficient visibility to navigate safely (typically 5 km or more, depending on airspace class)
- Remaining clear of clouds — you cannot fly into cloud under VFR

In simulation, this means choosing appropriate weather. If you spawn into fog or low cloud, you cannot legally (or safely) depart under VFR.

## Reading a VFR chart

VFR charts show:

| Feature | What to look for |
| --- | --- |
| Airspace boundaries | Coloured lines showing class of airspace and altitude limits |
| Control zones (CTR) | Circles around major airports — require ATC permission to enter |
| Danger areas | Marked with a D prefix — often active military airspace |
| Reporting points | Named positions used to navigate and report position to ATC |
| Obstructions | Towers, masts, and other hazards with height markings |

In MSFS and X-Plane, you can display VFR charts via **LittleNavMap** (see the [LittleNavMap guide](littlenavmap.md)) or third-party chart applications such as **SkyVector** (free, online).

## Altimetry

Altitude in aviation uses a **pressure altimeter** — an instrument that measures atmospheric pressure and converts it to altitude. The altimeter must be set correctly before flight.

| Setting | When to use |
| --- | --- |
| **QNH** | Regional pressure set at lower altitudes — altimeter reads height above sea level |
| **QFE** | Airfield pressure — altimeter reads height above the airfield (less common) |
| **Standard pressure (1013.25 hPa / 29.92 inHg)** | Used above the transition altitude — altimeter reads a flight level (FL) |

**Transition altitude** is the altitude below which QNH is used and above which standard pressure is used. It varies by country (typically 3,000–18,000 ft). In simulation, MSFS and X-Plane both model this.

## Traffic patterns

At uncontrolled airfields, you join the **traffic pattern** (also called the circuit) to organise your approach with other aircraft. A standard left-hand pattern consists of:

1. **Upwind** — after take-off, climbing straight ahead
2. **Crosswind** — turn 90° left, continue climbing
3. **Downwind** — parallel to the runway, opposite direction; reduce power, configure aircraft
4. **Base** — turn 90° toward the runway; deploy flaps, begin descent
5. **Final** — turn to align with the runway; fly the approach to landing

Always enter the pattern on the **upwind** or **45° to downwind** if arriving from outside.

## Communicating under VFR

At a controlled airport, you will need to contact the tower. A basic call on approach looks like:

> *"[Tower callsign], [your callsign], [aircraft type], [position], [altitude], [request]."*
> 
> Example: *"Manchester Tower, Golf-Alpha-Bravo, Cessna 172, 5 miles south, 2,000 feet, request landing."*

At uncontrolled fields, you make **blind calls** (position reports with no reply expected) on the Common Traffic Advisory Frequency (CTAF):

> *"[Airfield] Traffic, [callsign], [position in pattern], [runway], [airfield] Traffic."*
